## 1. Daily Practices, Content Creation, and Data Workflows

Growing up as a digital native, my relationship with technology has evolved from passive consumption to intentional curation and active creation. As an Interactive Media major with minors in Computer Science and Literature, my daily academic workflow relies on a matrix of specialized digital tools tailored for content creation, communication, and data management.

### Content Creation & Digital Storytelling

For visual design, interaction prototyping, and digital media production, my go-to ecosystem includes **Figma** for UI/UX wireframing, **Adobe Creative Cloud** (After Effects, Premiere Pro, and Photoshop) for motion graphics and video editing, and **p5.js / Processing** for generative art and interactive web experiences. Writing projects—whether essays or creative fiction—are typically drafted in **Markdown** using **Obsidian** or **VS Code**, allowing me to maintain modular, linked notes that connect literature concepts to media theory.

### Social Interaction & Collaborative Networks

Academic and personal collaboration spans synchronous and asynchronous platforms:

- **Discord & Slack:** Used for real-time channel-based community building, developer group discussions, and peer-to-peer troubleshooting.
- **GitHub:** Essential not only for code version control but also as a socio-technical platform where collaborative open-source contributions and peer code reviews take place.
- **Notion:** Acts as a shared workspace for team project tracking, digital boards, and shared documentation.

### Research Data Management & Data Analysis

When handling research data—whether textual corpuses from literature courses or user interaction logs from media experiments—I employ structured management and analysis workflows:

- **Data Organization & Storage:** Raw datasets are versioned using **Git** and backed up across **Zotero** (for academic reference management and PDF annotation) and cloud repositories.
- **Manipulation & Quantitative Analysis:** For structured tabular data, I use **Python (Pandas, NumPy)** alongside **Jupyter Notebooks** to clean, transform, and inspect datasets. Visualizations are built using **Matplotlib** and **Seaborn**.
- **Qualitative & Textual Data:** Text scraping and tokenization are performed using Python libraries like `NLTK` and `BeautifulSoup` to prepare text corpuses for computational analysis.

## 2. Navigating the Interdisciplinary Classroom

In our classroom, the diversity of academic backgrounds creates a rich tapestry of digital literacies. A Computer Science major might approach a problem through the lens of algorithmic efficiency, memory optimization, and formal logic. Conversely, a Literature or Creative Writing student brings deep narrative awareness, critical close-reading skills, and an acute sensitivity to historical context and nuance. Environmental Studies students view datasets as ecological systems and spatial relationships, while Film and New Media students analyze framing, temporal rhythm, and visual semiotics.

This intersection is where true digital literacy is tested. Digital literacy is not merely knowing how to write code or operate software; it is the meta-capacity to translate across these distinct domain languages.

### The Promise of Digital Humanities & Computational Thinking

Digital Humanities (DH) serves as a bridge between these worlds. By applying computational methods—such as topic modeling, network analysis of literary character interactions, or geospatial mapping of historical narratives—DH offers new horizons for how we study human culture. For a literature student, running a stylistic analysis script on 19th-century novels reveals patterns invisible to the naked eye. For a CS student, applying algorithms to messy, ambiguous historical texts demonstrates that real-world data is rarely neat or binary.

Collaborating across these fields expands our **computational thinking**. It forces us to decompose complex humanities problems into logical components without stripping away their human complexity. It teaches us abstraction, pattern recognition, and algorithm design in service of qualitative human questions.

### Approaching Data and AI Critically

As artificial intelligence and machine learning become ubiquitous, digital literacy demands critical engagement rather than blind adoption. In our interdisciplinary space, learning about AI goes beyond prompt engineering or API integration. It means examining dataset bias, training ethics, algorithmic transparency, and the environmental cost of large-scale computation. Interdisciplinary dialogue ensures that technical capability is constantly interrogated by ethical and cultural critique.

## 3. Critical Self-Reflection & Future Horizons

Reflecting on my current abilities, I recognize a strong foundational command of front-end development, media design, and basic scripting. However, this course has revealed significant gaps in my technical depth and conceptual frameworks. While I can comfortably write functional Python scripts for data manipulation, my understanding of back-end architecture, advanced statistical modeling, and machine learning principles remains surface-level.

### Discoveries in the Course

Throughout this course, I have discovered that data is never neutral; data collection is an act of interpretation. Learning to view code as a rhetorical medium—one that conveys values, power dynamics, and structural biases—has reshaped how I approach software design. I have also realized how much efficiency can be gained by automating repetitive research tasks, freeing up cognitive space for higher-level analysis.

### New Ways of Thinking to Explore

Moving forward, I want to explore several new trajectories:

1. **Deeper Machine Learning Applications in DH:** I aim to move beyond basic text processing to train customized small-scale models on historical text and visual archives.
2. **Critical Algorithm Studies:** Integrating algorithmic auditing into my development process to actively measure and mitigate bias in interactive applications.
3. **Physical Computing & Environmental Data:** Bridging interactive media with environmental sensors to create physical data visualizations that make abstract climate metrics tangible.

Ultimately, digital literacy is an ongoing, reflective practice. By combining computational rigor with humanistic inquiry, I hope to contribute to tech-enabled spaces that are not only innovative, but deeply ethical, contextual, and human-centered.
