For this assignment, we chose to work with Lorimer’s Gazetteer of the Persian Gulf, Oman and Central Arabia to explore the following research question: **How many houses does the Gazetteer record in the Trucial States?** This question emerged as we examined the source material and the accompanying [original CSV dataset]({{ '/assets/images/assignment2/data/lorimer-gazetteer-1-0.csv' | relative_url }}). References to houses offered a way to investigate the settlements described in the text and compare their recorded sizes.

Our aim was to extract the relevant information and present it through an interactive map. Mapping the data therefore would allow us to examine the geographical distribution of the recorded houses alongside their numbers and concentration.

However, answering this question required more than simply plotting the available data. We also needed to establish a geographical area of interest, filter out unrelated locations, and extract house counts from the relevant text. The project therefore involved several stages of data preparation, mapping, and analysis that will be showcased here.

# Process

To begin, we identified two main requirements:

1. Obtain the [original CSV dataset]({{ '/assets/images/assignment2/data/lorimer-gazetteer-1-0.csv' | relative_url }}) containing the relevant locations and references to houses from the source material.

2. Select a mapping platform that would allow us to display and explore these records interactively.

We chose [kepler.gl](https://kepler.gl), which we used through Python. To create an initial version of the map, we asked GitHub Copilot to generate a Python template that could load the dataset and plot its geographical coordinates. This automated part of the setup allowed us to move more quickly to examining the results. We then checked the initial output to assess whether the displayed locations matched the intended scope of our research.

The first test revealed a significant problem: the map displayed records from across the Persian Gulf region, rather than only those relevant to the Trucial States. This made the map difficult to interpret for our research question and showed that we needed to narrow the dataset geographically.

After discussing this issue with our professor, we were advised to use a **bounding box**. A bounding box defines a rectangular area using minimum and maximum latitude and longitude coordinates. Therefore in order to do this, we used [Klokan’s Bounding Box Tool](https://boundingbox.klokantech.com/) to select an area around the present-day United Arab Emirates as an initial geographical filter.

We then incorporated the GeoJSON generated by the tool into our Python mapping workflow. However, the next version of the map continued to display locations outside the selected area. This indicated that adding the boundary to the map had not successfully restricted the underlying records being plotted, despite telling the LLM to limit the number of houses to that box.

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/assignment2/secondtest.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Second map test showing houses outside the selected boundary box">

</figure>

To address this, we asked Copilot to help produce a [filtered CSV]({{ '/assets/images/assignment2/data/lorimer-gazetteer-bounded.csv' | relative_url }}) containing only records whose coordinates fell within the bounding box. Filtering the dataset itself allowed us to limit the points displayed on the map and made the resulting visualization more relevant to our area of interest.

Once we had created this smaller dataset, we asked Claude to analyse the associated text and extract the recorded numbers of houses. The resulting [houses CSV]({{ '/assets/images/assignment2/data/lorimer-gazetteer-houses.csv' | relative_url }}) combines the mapped locations with those extracted values. This stage connected the geographical records with the numerical information needed to address our research question.

An important limitation of this approach is that a rectangular bounding box around the present-day UAE does not precisely define the historical Trucial States. The geographical filter therefore provides a practical starting point, but the included locations still require historical verification.

We found that the most densely populated area was along the coast. Namely, Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Sharjah, and Ras Al Khaimah being the most obvious examples. There is one clear exception: Liwa, which is the arc shaped points deep inland, is still populated albeit not as dense as the aforementioned cities.

# Discussion

We believe that the reasons for the most densely populated areas being along the coastline are pearl fishing, fishing for food, and trade. Pearl fishing was the primary source of income on publication of the Gazetteer in 1915 (Lorimer 1438). Pearl fishing being a source of income attracts people in the region to join and perhaps make an income. With the pearls came trade which may have also been a source of food. An additional source of food would be fishing (1440). Altogether with pearl trading attracting people and fishing and trade supplying the food to feed the influx of people that could explain why it’s populated and other places in the region are not.

As for Liwa, the houses there could be explained by there being fertile land (Harris). Because it’s not as densely populated but there is still a rural population fertile land and hence farming would explain how people could live there. But, with farming being the only source of food it wouldn’t be able to support a larger population.

# Limitations

Lorimer’s Gazetteer is not without its limitations and the same is true of our methodology. Firstly, the Gazetteer is not a comprehensive survey of houses in the trucial states rather it looks at the broader gulf which means there may be villages, families, or some houses that were missed. In addition, the number of houses for certain families were estimated or had a large range. As for our methodology, an LLM (Claude in this case) is not going to be perfect. The LLM could examine the more than 2000 pages in roughly 10 minutes however we cannot verify that it associated the right number of houses with the right families. Fortunately, the PDF of the Gazetteer had text which could be read directly by the LLM and did not need an OCR with a few exceptions (in which case Tesseract was used), but that’s not perfect either.

This would fall directly into what has been taught to us about the dangers of distant reading, in the sense that we will not get all the information that might be understood if we carefully read each page. However, realistically reading around 2,000 pages as a student could be considered almost impossible.

However, when it comes to analysis, we encountered that we had to engage in a lot of Distant Reading with the usage of LLMs, therefore maybe leaving some room for errors that we might not be very aware of.
