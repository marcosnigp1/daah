# Introduction

For this assignment, we chose to work with Lorimer’s Gazetteer of the Persian Gulf, Oman and Central Arabia to explore the following research question: How many houses does the **Gazetteer record in the Trucial States?** This question emerged as we examined the source material and the accompanying CSV dataset. References to houses offered a way to investigate the settlements described in the text and compare their recorded sizes.

Our aim was to extract the relevant information and present it through an interactive map. Mapping the data therefore would allow us to examine the geographical distribution of the recorded houses alongside their numbers and concentration.

However, answering this question required more than simply plotting the available data. We also needed to establish a geographical area of interest, filter out unrelated locations, and extract house counts from the relevant text. The project therefore involved several stages of data preparation, mapping, and analysis that will be showcased here.

# Process

To begin, we identified two main requirements:

1. Obtain the CSV dataset containing the relevant locations and references to houses from the source material.

2. Select a mapping platform that would allow us to display and explore these records interactively.

We chose [kepler.gl](https://kepler.gl), which we used through Python. To create an initial version of the map, we asked GitHub Copilot to generate a Python template that could load the dataset and plot its geographical coordinates. This automated part of the setup allowed us to move more quickly to examining the results. We then checked the initial output to assess whether the displayed locations matched the intended scope of our research.

The first test revealed a significant problem: the map displayed records from across the Persian Gulf region, rather than only those relevant to the Trucial States. This made the map difficult to interpret for our research question and showed that we needed to narrow the dataset geographically.

After discussing this issue with our professor, we were advised to use a **bounding box**. A bounding box defines a rectangular area using minimum and maximum latitude and longitude coordinates. Therefore in order to do this, we used [Klokan’s Bounding Box Tool](https://boundingbox.klokantech.com/) to select an area around the present-day United Arab Emirates as an initial geographical filter.

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/assignment2/settingboundarybox.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Klokan Bounding Box Tool showing the selected boundary">
  <figcaption>Figure 2. Preparing the boundary box in Klokan.</figcaption>
</figure>

We then incorporated the GeoJSON generated by the tool into our Python mapping workflow. However, the next version of the map continued to display locations outside the selected area. This indicated that adding the boundary to the map had not successfully restricted the underlying records being plotted, despite telling the LLM to limit the number of houses to that box.

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/assignment2/secondtest.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Second map test showing houses outside the selected boundary box">
  <figcaption>Figure 3. The houses are still everywhere despite having the boundary box set.</figcaption>
</figure>

To address this, we asked Copilot to help produce a filtered CSV containing only records whose coordinates fell within the bounding box. Filtering the dataset itself allowed us to limit the points displayed on the map and made the resulting visualization more relevant to our area of interest.

Once we had created this smaller dataset, we asked Claude to analyse the associated text and extract the recorded numbers of houses. This stage connected the geographical records with the numerical information needed to address our research question.

An important limitation of this approach is that a rectangular bounding box around the present-day UAE does not precisely define the historical Trucial States. The geographical filter therefore provides a practical starting point, but the included locations still require historical verification.
