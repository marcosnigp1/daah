# Reflection on Digital Literacy

As a 20-year-old university student majoring in Interactive Media, I have realized that digital literacy is something I use almost every day, even when I do not consciously think about it. A large part of my education involves working with digital tools, creating media, researching information, and communicating with other people. Because of this, I often think of myself as digitally literate. However, this course has made me realize that digital literacy is not only about knowing how to use software. It is also about understanding how digital information is created, organized, interpreted, and shared.

In my daily and academic life, I already use different strategies for creating and communicating digitally. I use platforms such as Google Docs and Microsoft Office for writing and organizing assignments, while Discord, WhatsApp, and email are important for communicating with classmates. For content creation, I have experience with software such as Unity, Blender, TouchDesigner, and image editing tools. These programs allow me to create interactive experiences, 3D environments, visuals, and other forms of digital media. I have also learned that creating something digitally is not simply about knowing which buttons to press. It involves making decisions about how information should be presented and how another person will experience it.

Research is another area where digital literacy is important. I use databases, Google Scholar, online articles, and other digital resources to find information for my assignments and projects. I also work with tools such as Excel or Google Sheets when organizing data, and I have used ArcGIS for mapping and presenting information. These tools have shown me that data management is often just as important as the final result. If information is poorly organized, incomplete, or interpreted incorrectly, even a visually impressive project can communicate something misleading.

One thing I find particularly interesting about our class is the range of majors represented in the room. Someone studying Computer Science might approach a problem through programming and algorithms, while someone studying Film and New Media might think more about visual storytelling. An Environmental Studies student might work with environmental datasets, while a Literature or Creative Writing student might focus on language, narratives, and cultural interpretation. As an Interactive Media student, I naturally tend to think about interaction and experience. These differences can sometimes make collaboration more difficult, but they also create opportunities to learn approaches that I would not normally encounter.

This is where I think digital humanities can be especially interesting. Digital humanities can connect fields that might initially seem very different by using computational tools to study subjects such as literature, history, culture, art, and society. It makes me think about how I could approach questions that are normally explored through qualitative methods using data, visualization, mapping, or interactive media. At the same time, it reminds me that data cannot always explain everything. Numbers can show patterns, but they do not necessarily explain the human experiences behind those patterns.

Collaboration can also help develop my computational thinking. I do not think computational thinking is limited to becoming a programmer. It can involve breaking a large problem into smaller parts, identifying patterns, organizing information, and thinking about how different elements interact. Working with people from different disciplines can force me to explain my own way of thinking while also learning how other people approach the same problem. Someone else's method might reveal a solution that I would not have considered on my own.

Artificial intelligence adds another layer to digital literacy. I already interact with AI tools, but this course has encouraged me to think more critically about what they produce and how I use them. I do not want to treat AI as a replacement for my own thinking. Instead, I see it as another tool that can help me explore ideas, organize information, or approach a problem from a different perspective. At the same time, I need to be aware of problems such as inaccurate information, bias, authorship, and overreliance on generated content.

Overall, I think my digital literacy is strong in some areas but still developing in others. I am comfortable creating digital media and learning new software, but I want to become better at working with data and understanding computational approaches to research. I also want to explore how digital tools can change the way I understand subjects outside of Interactive Media. More than simply learning new software, I am interested in learning new ways of thinking. I think that is one of the most valuable parts of digital literacy: being able to look at the same problem through different digital and disciplinary perspectives.
