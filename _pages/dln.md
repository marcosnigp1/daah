As a 22-year-old university student majoring in Interactive Media, I have realized that digital literacy is something I use almost every day, even when I do not consciously think about it. A large part of my education involves working with digital tools, creating media, researching information, and communicating with other people. However, this course has made me realize that digital literacy is not only about knowing how to use software. It is also about understanding how digital information is created, organized, interpreted, and shared across time and history.

In my daily and academic life, I use different tools for creating and communicating digitally. Such tools are the Google Suite, Microsoft Suite and various open source software for writing and organizing assignments. For content creation, I have experience with software such as Unity, Blender, TouchDesigner, and image editing tools. I also know some very niche software like Ultimate Doom Builder, a mapping tool for the older Doom games. These programs in general allow me to create interactive experiences, 3D environments, visuals, and other forms of digital experienecs. I have also learned that creating something digitally is not simply about knowing which buttons to press, it is about trying to envoke emotions.

Research is another area where digital literacy is important. Generally for this, I use public and private databases, especially NYU's library since as the time of writing this, I am a student there. I also use Google Scholar, online articles, and other digital resources to find information for my assignments and projects. In order to arrange data, I also work with tools such as Excel, Google Sheets or even automatized Python scripts that help me quickly organize data. For mapping, so far I have used ArcGIS, since it helps a lot to present the information. I believe that if the information is not presented in an easy way and simple, people will just not look at it. Everything has to be as entertaining and interesting as possible while respecting people's time.

One thing I find particularly interesting about our class is how interactive the lessons are. What I mean by this is that, we are not only just watching the screen, we are also engaging deeply in the contents that are being taught. For example, while the professor is explaining a core concept, we at the same time, are engaging with it by interacting with the displayed websites, or coding! (my favorite thing). Therefore, making us engage with the concepts not only theoritically, but also practically (something I wish more professors did).

Digital humanities can be interesting. This particular field can connect fields that might seem very different to each other, for example: What does themes in literature have to do with War? but by using computational tools to study such subjects, we could find an bigger (possibly unaswered) answer. It makes me think about how I could approach questions that are normally explored through qualitative methods using data, visualization or even mapping. Although, it is important to remember that while numbers can show patterns, they do not necessarily explain the human experiences behind those patterns.

Collaboration can also help develop my computational thinking. I do not think computational thinking is limited to becoming a programmer. It can involve breaking a large problem into smaller parts, identifying patterns, organizing information, and thinking about how different elements interact. Working with people from different disciplines can force me to explain my own way of thinking while also learning how other people approach the same problem. Someone else's method might reveal a solution that I would not have considered on my own.

Artificial intelligence adds another layer to digital literacy. I already interact with AI tools, but this course has encouraged me to think more critically about what they produce and how I use them. I do not want to treat AI as a replacement for my own thinking. Instead, I see it as another tool that can help me explore ideas, organize information, or approach a problem from a different perspective. At the same time, I need to be aware of problems such as inaccurate information, bias, authorship, and overreliance on generated content.

Overall, I think my digital literacy is strong in some areas but still developing in others. I am comfortable creating digital media and learning new software, but I want to become better at working with data and understanding computational approaches to research. I also want to explore how digital tools can change the way I understand subjects outside of Interactive Media. More than simply learning new software, I am interested in learning new ways of thinking. I think that is one of the most valuable parts of digital literacy: being able to look at the same problem through different digital and disciplinary perspectives.
